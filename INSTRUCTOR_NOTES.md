# Instructor notes

The matrices are teaching constructions modeled on the logic of Tajfel, Billig, Bundy, & Flament (1971, *European Journal of Social Psychology, 1*(2), 149-178). They are **not** the original booklet values; don't call them "Tajfel's matrices." Group assignment is announced as random, as in Billig & Tajfel (1973, *EJSP, 3*(1), 27-52), where random categorization was still enough to produce ingroup-favoring allocations.

## ~15-minute run

1. (Before class) Reset data; confirm `n = 0`; results hidden.
2. (2 min) Project the dashboard (results hidden). Students scan the QR code. Say only that groups are random and there are no right answers. Don't describe strategies.
3. (5 min) Students complete both matrices. Watch `n` climb. Close submissions when it plateaus.
4. (3 min) Reveal. Walk through in this order: histograms (A, B) → mean difference B − A with CI → scatter → color check → order check.
5. (5 min) Debrief.

## How the matrices work

Lean = 7 − c (−6..+6; 0 = fair), identical in A and B. Each card gives points to one ingroup and one outgroup person; the student gets none.

| Column | Matrix A (in/out) | Matrix B (in/out) |
|---|---|---|
| c = 1 (lean +6) | 7 / 1 | 19 / 7 |
| c = 7 (lean 0) | 13 / 13 | 13 / 13 |
| c = 13 (lean −6) | 19 / 25 | 7 / 19 |

- **B (free):** total fixed at 26, so favoring the ingroup costs nothing.
- **A (costly):** moving toward positive lean widens the ingroup advantage but *lowers both groups' points* (ingroup 13 → 7). **Interpretive trap:** the negative-lean end of A (19/25) gives the ingroup its *highest absolute* and the highest joint points. So a negative A lean is not "favoring the outgroup" in a motivational sense; it is what profit-maximizing choosers pick. Mean A lean mixes differentiation, fairness, and profit motives. B is the cleaner measure of favoritism.

## What to expect (not guaranteed; n ≈ 200, one section)

- A plurality or many fair choices (lean 0), especially in B.
- A modest average lean toward the ingroup, with a wide spread.
- More lean in B than A (B − A > 0), since favoring costs nothing in B. A positive B − A is the demonstration of the cost difference; treat as a prediction and let the CI speak.
- Color means both ≥ 0 (each group favors itself); order effect small. With one class these will be noisy; say so.

## Debrief prompts

- Why would anyone give up points (A, positive lean) to widen the gap? What other goals could produce that choice (relative advantage vs. fairness vs. maximizing profit)?
- Why is the A distribution harder to interpret than B?
- Groups were random and meaningless. What does that imply about what is needed to produce bias?
- What control condition would you add? (A no-groups condition; a condition where the recipients are two ingroup or two outgroup members; anonymous vs. observed responding.)

## Caveats to state out loud

- The effect is a shift toward the ingroup (favoritism), not hostility. It is robust (Mullen, Brown, & Smith, 1992, *EJSP, 22*, 103-122; Balliet, Wu, & De Dreu, 2014, *Psychological Bulletin, 140*, 1556-1581), but weaker for negative than for positive allocations.
- There is no no-groups control, so you can't tell how much of a lean is due to group labels versus other features of the task.
- Students know the instructor is watching and may respond to demand characteristics; the same two recipients appear in both matrices, so the two choices are not independent evidence about unrelated people.
- This is a classroom demonstration with one section; don't generalize from it.
